Skeleton Second Line: A Pure CSS Jazz Funeral

A Little Magic from Tappable Stories

This pen grew out of something I’ve been building at Tappable Stories, where I’m exploring new ways to bring history and storytelling to life.

One of my favorite techniques is layering interactive elements over video, allowing photographs, characters, documents, and stories to appear right where they belong, without interrupting the experience.

For this CodePen, I decided to have a little fun with that same idea.

Instead of bringing history to life, I’m bringing the dead to life!

Welcome to the Big Easy. Even the Dead Know How to Party.

After midnight at New Orleans’ Metairie Cemetery, the tomb doors swing open and the Bone Rattlers Social Aid & Pleasure Club comes marching out.

Six krewes. One brass band. A coffin full of trouble. And enough bats, bones, and bad behavior to wake the dead.

No JavaScript. Just CSS, a little voodoo, and a whole lotta New Orleans.

Hit START THE PARADE, crank up the volume, and CUT HIM LOOSE!

How the Dead Get Movin’

One click wakes the cemetery.

The START button is a label connected to a hidden checkbox. CSS :has() detects when it’s checked and brings the parade to life. No JavaScript required.

The video becomes the stage.

Everything lives inside the same 16:9 frame. Skeletons, bats, crows, and other surprises are positioned using percentages measured against the video itself. That keeps the characters marching along the cemetery paths, even when the screen changes size.

The skeletons come right at you.

Each character follows a carefully timed CSS animation, moving out of the tomb, growing larger as it approaches, then disappearing offscreen. A little scaling and perspective turn flat images into a marching parade.

Every bone has its own rhythm.

CSS animation delays control when each skeleton, bat, and crow appears. They’re choreographed to the soundtrack: each krewe steps out of its tomb the moment the song calls its name, right down to the final surprise.

Music without JavaScript? Well, almost magic.

CSS can’t play audio directly, so the music lives inside a separate HTML page loaded through a lazy-loading iframe. Starting the parade reveals the iframe and triggers the soundtrack. STOP THE BAND switches to a silent page.

And when the party gets too wild…

It doesn’t stop. There’s no pause button: CSS can’t pause the music, and a parade that froze while the band played on would just fall out of step. Even the dead can’t stop a New Orleans brass band! STOP THE BAND turns the music off, RESTART starts the whole show over, and HIDE CAPTIONS, a second checkbox, tucks the captions away without losing their place.

What’s in the Files?

I’ve separated the main pieces so you can poke around without digging through the whole cemetery.

  • index.html — The cemetery, characters, parade structure, and controls. This is where the whole production comes together.
  • style.css — The stage, video positioning, interface, buttons, and CSS controls that start the show.
  • parade.css — Where the real mischief happens: character positions, marching paths, scaling, timing, and special effects.
  • captions.css — Closed captions for the dead: comedy captions cued to the song and the action, and HIDE CAPTIONS tucks them away without losing the beat.
  • song.html — The little music player that starts the soundtrack.
  • song-off.html — A silent page used to stop the band.
  • script.js — Completely empty. That’s right. Not a single line of JavaScript.
  • videos/metairie-cemetery.mp4 — The looping cemetery background that serves as our stage.
  • haunt/reaper-scythe.svg — The Grim Reaper, borrowed from the Demon Killer game, who closes the show.
  • backgrounds/metairie-cemetery-poster.webp — The poster image displayed while the video loads.
  • music/rattle-them-bones.mp3 — The original soundtrack, created with Suno.
  • screenshot.jpg — A preview of the production.
